# Chapter 4 — Why the Box Works

## The Trouble With Intelligence

There is a particular danger in becoming reasonably good at figuring things out.

Eventually, you begin to expect it of yourself.

I've spent enough years engineering, fabricating, troubleshooting, reverse-engineering, and generally refusing to leave malfunctioning objects alone that I've developed a strange sense of responsibility about it. With an IQ somewhere around 140 and a brain that seems unusually comfortable dismantling complicated systems into understandable pieces, failing to use that ability feels almost negligent.

If something is broken, I want to know why.

If nobody knows why, now I'm interested.

If the appropriate tool doesn't exist, I'll probably try to make one.

If somebody tells me the thing can't be done, there's a fair chance they've just ruined my afternoon, because now I have to find out.

Somewhere along the way I apparently decided that this was my way of being responsible with whatever intellectual horsepower I happened to get.

This sounds admirable until you examine the operator.

Because despite all of this, I remain completely incapable of reliably going to the bathroom when I realize I have to piss.

The sequence is remarkably consistent.

*I have to pee.*

Then something catches my attention.

Ten minutes later:

*Goddammit, I still have to pee.*

This is irritating, because I distinctly remember already solving this problem conceptually.

So I make a firmer mental note.

*Okay. Finish this one thing. Then go piss.*

Apparently this constitutes closure as far as my brain is concerned.

Twenty minutes later my bladder submits another support ticket.

**URGENT: PREVIOUS ISSUE REMAINS UNRESOLVED.**

And I'm annoyed.

Not with the bladder.

With myself.

Because this is now the third time I've discovered the same problem, correctly diagnosed its cause, identified the appropriate corrective action, and then failed to implement the repair.

At some point the situation becomes philosophically offensive.

Here is a brain capable of following electrical signals through a circuit, visualizing mechanical forces through structures that don't exist yet, juggling several competing explanations for a failure, and revising those explanations as new evidence arrives.

Meanwhile, the same brain is being repeatedly outmaneuvered by a bladder.

Not an unfamiliar bladder, either.

Mine.

I've had it the entire time.

This is where the joke becomes inconvenient.

We tend to imagine intelligence as though it were a general-purpose upgrade to the human mind. More intelligence, better thinking. Better thinking, fewer stupid mistakes.

But intelligence isn't a view from outside the system.

It's horsepower inside it.

It can process relationships, detect patterns, construct models, test explanations and extrapolate consequences with remarkable power. But it still operates on whatever information has reached its attention, whatever assumptions it has inherited, and whatever priorities the rest of the system has decided matter at that particular moment.

A powerful mind can reason beautifully from a terrible premise.

It can overlook an obvious fact.

It can construct an elaborate explanation for something simple.

And perhaps most dangerously, it can become so impressed by the sophistication of its reasoning that it forgets to inspect what the reasoning started with.

That's the Box.

The Box doesn't disappear because you're intelligent.

Intelligence just gives you better equipment inside it.

And sometimes that equipment is spectacular. We split atoms. We detected gravitational waves. We reconstructed the history of life from molecules. We put machines on other planets and instructed them to conduct geology on our behalf.

Then one of the people capable of understanding how some of those things work stands up suddenly and realizes he's been needing to piss for forty-five minutes.

There is something wonderfully humiliating about that.

Perhaps humility doesn't require us to think less of human intelligence.

Perhaps it requires us to understand what intelligence actually is.

It is not omniscience in miniature.

It is an extraordinarily capable reasoning system operating from a limited position, with incomplete information, competing priorities, inherited assumptions, imperfect attention, and absolutely no guarantee that the most obvious thing in the room has made it into the calculation.

That distinction matters enormously.

Because if a mind can repeatedly lose track of information being transmitted directly from an organ physically attached to it, perhaps we should exercise a little caution before assuming that the boundaries of that mind correspond neatly to the boundaries of the universe.

This does not mean anything can be true.

It means intelligence deserves respect without worship.

And whenever I become tempted to forget that, fortunately, my body has installed a recurring reminder.

Usually about ten minutes after the previous one.
